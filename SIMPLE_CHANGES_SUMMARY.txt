PART 1 -- NEW TOOLS WE USED THAT THE PAPER DOES NOT MENTION
================================================================================
Add these to Section II.B (Research Instrument).

NetSurfP-3.0
  Predicts the shape of small parts of a protein (helix, sheet, or loose coil)
  from its sequence. We used it because RaptorX, the tool in our paper, is
  offline.

IEDB Query API
  A database of epitopes that scientists have already tested in a lab. We use
  it to check if our predicted epitopes have real experimental proof.

AFND (Allele Frequency Net Database)
  Lists how common each HLA gene type is in different populations. We need this
  to compute how many people our vaccine would cover.

PDBFixer
  Repairs broken or missing atoms in a 3D protein file before we analyze it.

OpenMM
  Relaxes the 3D model so the atoms sit at comfortable distances instead of
  crashing into each other.

FreeSASA
  Measures how much of the protein surface is exposed to water, and how much of
  that exposed surface is oily.

pydssp
  Reads the finished 3D model and reports the shape of each part. Our paper
  says we measure this but never names the tool.

Bio.PDB.Superimposer
  Overlaps two 3D models and measures how far apart they are. We use it to test
  whether AlphaFold's five models agree with each other.

CaBLAM (part of Phenix)
  Checks whether the protein backbone is bent into believable shapes. It catches
  problems that the normal MolProbity score misses.

BLAST+ (blastp and makeblastdb)
  Compares our peptides against big protein databases to find look-alikes. We
  built two databases on our own computer: known toxins, and all human proteins.

RCSB REST API
  Downloads structure files and their records from the Protein Data Bank
  automatically, instead of copying by hand.

ALSO FIX: our paper says Python 3.14. We actually used Python 3.11 for
everything except Aggrescan3D, which only runs on Python 2.7.

PART 2 -- TOOLS IN THE PAPER THAT WE SWAPPED OR COULD NOT USE
================================================================================

RaptorX  ->  NetSurfP-3.0                                    [deviation #16]
  RaptorX predicts protein shape from sequence. Its server is down and returns
  an error every time. NetSurfP-3.0 does the same job and works, so we used it
  instead.

IEDB allele tables  ->  AFND                                  [deviation #7]
  Both list how common each HLA gene type is in a population. We switched
  because we needed a specific population and IEDB did not have usable data for
  it. This is the most important wording fix in the whole paper -- see Part 4.

SEMA 2.0  ->  did not run                                     [deviation #20]
  SEMA 2.0 predicts B-cell epitopes that form from folded shapes rather than
  straight sequence. Its website blocks any computer program that is not a real
  human browser, and we could not get past it. This only means we are missing
  extra proof; it did not change a single epitope in our vaccine.

ToxinPred and HemoPI  ->  ToxinPred2 and HemoPI2
  These predict if a peptide is toxic or destroys red blood cells. We used the
  newer version 2 of each, installed on our own computer instead of the
  website. Same research group, same method, just newer.

FoldX web  ->  FoldX 5 installed locally
  FoldX estimates how much energy holds a protein together. We ran the real
  program on our computer instead of the website version.

"ExPASy Protulation"
  This looks like a typo for ProtParam. It also says ProtParam gives RMSD and
  RMSF, but those come from the movement simulation in Phase III, not from
  ProtParam. This sentence needs rewriting.

PART 3 -- NEW STEPS THAT HAVE NO SECTION IN THE PAPER
================================================================================

NEW STEP 1H -- checking our epitopes against real lab results   [deviation #22]
  Every epitope in our vaccine was chosen by prediction software. Nothing in
  our paper ever asks whether any of them has actually been tested in a
  laboratory, so we added a step that checks all 32 against IEDB's records of
  real experiments. The result is good for us: 26 of 32 (81%) have some kind of
  published experimental support, and 10 of those are strong matches with real
  assay data attached. Two things must be said alongside that number. First, 14
  of the 26 matches come from vaccinia virus, a close relative of mpox, not
  from mpox itself -- we kept them because that same family relationship is why
  the smallpox vaccine protects against mpox, but they must be labelled as
  cross-species. Second, we double-checked every match against our own source
  proteins, because a short 9-letter peptide can match by pure luck in a large
  virus. All 26 passed that check. The 6 with no match are not a problem;
  finding new epitopes is the whole point of prediction.

NEW -- total population coverage, not just per-epitope        [deviation #23]
  Our paper measures coverage one epitope at a time and asks each to reach 90%.
  That is a fair way to rank epitopes, but it is not the vaccine's coverage. A
  person is protected if ANY epitope in the vaccine matches one of their HLA
  types, so the right number combines all epitopes together. The difference is
  huge and it favors us: alone, only 4 of 23 epitopes reach 90%, but together
  they cover over 99.99% of the test population. We added this calculation and
  it uses the exact same formula already in our paper, just applied to the
  whole set instead of one peptide at a time.

NEW -- checking how confident AlphaFold is in its own model    [deviation #21]
  Our paper checks the 3D model with MolProbity only. MolProbity checks whether
  the small details are correct -- bond lengths, angles, atoms not overlapping.
  It does not check whether the overall folded shape is right, and a completely
  shapeless protein with tidy details will still pass it. So we added a check
  using AlphaFold's own confidence scores. This turned out to be the most
  important thing we found, and it changes what Phase III is allowed to claim.
  See Part 4.

NEW -- preparing the receptor files before docking
  Our paper just says we take TLR-2 (6NIG) and TLR-4 (8WTA) from the Protein
  Data Bank and dock them. Three problems in those raw files would ruin the
  docking and none is mentioned. First, the 6NIG file is not pure human TLR-2:
  scientists glued a piece of hagfish protein onto it to help it crystallize,
  and if we do not cut that off, our vaccine could dock onto hagfish protein
  and we would report it as human. Second, 6NIG already has a small drug
  molecule (Diprovocim) sitting inside the exact pocket we want our vaccine to
  bind to, so it must be removed or it blocks the spot. Third, HADDOCK cannot
  read the file format these come in, so everything has to be converted. We
  wrote a step that handles all three and records what it removed and why.

PART 4 -- WHERE OUR RESULTS DO NOT MATCH WHAT THE PAPER SAYS
================================================================================
These are not additions. The paper currently says things our data does not
support, and the text has to change.

"PHILIPPINE POPULATION COVERAGE" IS NOT WHAT WE COMPUTED      [deviation #7]
  Our virus strains really are Philippine -- CRF01_AE and Clade II. But our HLA
  coverage is not. AFND has no Philippine data we could use: the Metro Manila
  set is too rough (it says A*02 instead of A*02:01), the only detailed
  Philippine set is from an indigenous Batanes group that does not represent
  lowland Filipinos, and two of the six HLA genes have no Philippine data at
  all. We used Singapore Riau Malay instead, which is the closest usable match
  because it is the same Austronesian ancestry. Everywhere the paper says
  "Philippine population coverage," it must say "Southeast Asian (Austronesian)
  proxy coverage."

WE CLAIM SEVEN ANTIGENS BUT ONLY SIX ARE TRULY REPRESENTED
  HIV p17 contributed no epitope of its own -- its two epitopes are shared with
  p24, because both are parts of the same larger Gag protein. That is fine, but
  we should say it. The bigger issue is that HIV gp41 has ZERO B-cell epitopes.
  That matters because gp41 is where the most famous HIV antibodies bind, and a
  reviewer will notice. The reason is now known exactly: all of gp41's B-cell
  candidates overlap epitopes we already picked from gp41 for the T-cell slots,
  and our rule blocks using the same region twice. That rule is correct -- we
  should not count one spot twice -- but it means gp41's ten candidate slots
  were spent on only two regions. Fixing this needs a diversity rule plus about
  50-100 more manual website submissions, and we did not do it. State it as a
  limitation.

WE CANNOT DOCK THE WHOLE VACCINE, ONLY THE ADJUVANT           [deviation #21]
  Our paper says Phase III docks the whole AlphaFold model into TLR-2 and
  TLR-4. The numbers say we cannot. AlphaFold is confident about only 46 of the
  566 amino acids, and those 46 are exactly the beta-defensin-3 adjuvant at the
  front. For everything else, 84.6% of the amino acids score below 50 out of
  100 confidence, and when we compare AlphaFold's five different attempts they
  disagree by 18 to 24 Angstroms -- meaning the model does not know where those
  parts go. Docking that would just be docking one random guess, and anyone
  repeating it with a different model file would get a different answer. This
  is NOT a mistake in our design. We chose stretchy linkers on purpose so each
  epitope stays loose and reachable; a locked-in shape was never the goal, and
  no amount of extra simulation will fold it. So Phase III docks the adjuvant
  only. This is not settling for less: beta-defensin 3 IS the part that
  triggers TLR-2 and TLR-4, which is the entire reason it is in the vaccine.
  The part AlphaFold is sure about is exactly the part the docking claim needs.
  This also affects research questions 4, 5 and 6, and the Phase IV split-
  construct tests.

MOLPROBITY ALONE DOES NOT PROVE THE STRUCTURE IS GOOD
  We pass what the paper asks for: 97.16% of amino acids in favored
  Ramachandran regions (target 95-98%) and a MolProbity score of 0.90 (0.5-1.5
  is excellent). But the full MolProbity report also shows four red rows we
  should report: favored side-chain shapes 91.54% (goal above 98%), backbone
  geometry problems 2.31% (goal below 0.5%), CaBLAM problems 3.2% (goal below
  1%), and 2 unusual bond types. The MolProbity score does not include any of
  those, which is why it still looks good. When we mapped where those problems
  are, 59 of 62 sit outside the adjuvant in the exact low-confidence regions
  described above. So this is a fourth separate tool telling us the same thing:
  the small details are excellent, the overall shape is not settled.

OUR VACCINE IS "PREDICTED INSOLUBLE" AND WE KEPT IT ANYWAY    [deviation #15]
  Our paper says clearly that anything predicted insoluble was rejected. Our
  vaccine scores 0.444 on DeepSol, which counts as insoluble, and we kept it.
  This is our weakest point and the sentence must be rewritten. The honest
  defense is that our two solubility predictors disagree: CamSol scores the
  same sequence 0.865, which is comfortably soluble and the best score of any
  version we built. The 3D tools do not back up DeepSol either -- both sticky
  patches they found sit in the floppy regions AlphaFold is unsure about. Under
  our paper's own combined rule (reject only if DeepSol fails AND a 3D tool
  finds a big sticky patch in a confident region), our vaccine is NOT rejected.
  We should cite that combined rule as the real one. Also worth noting: making
  the vaccine shorter does not help. A 393-amino-acid test version scored
  0.393, which is worse.

THE OILY-SURFACE WARNINGS ARE ABOUT FLOPPINESS, NOT STICKINESS  [deviation #18]
  Our paper flags a vaccine if more than 25% of its surface is oily or if the
  oily patch is bigger than 250 square Angstroms. We measure 40.9% and 649. But
  both numbers are measured from a 3D model that is 84.6% floppy, so they
  describe one random pose of a shapeless chain, not a real fixed surface. This
  should be reported as more evidence of floppiness, not as a stickiness
  problem.

SMALLER NUMBER FIXES
  We planned 30 sequences per protein and got 202 of 210. Mpox A35R gave 27 and
  B5R gave 25 because NCBI simply does not have more. Our ESM-2 model was
  trained on 774 real sequences (390 antigenic, 384 not), not the 500 + 500 the
  paper states -- we chose real data over padding the number with fake
  sequences. Also, our antigenicity step passed 167 out of 167 with zero
  disagreement between the two methods, which means it did not filter anything.
  That is expected since all seven proteins are known to trigger immunity, so
  we should present it as two methods agreeing with each other, not as a filter.

PART 5 -- RULES WE CHANGED
================================================================================
One short paragraph each. Full reasoning is in the deviations file.

#1  TOXICITY -- we now need proof, not just a score
    The raw ToxinPred score alone labelled human albumin, human GAPDH, a plain
    linker, and even our own beta-defensin-3 adjuvant as "toxic," which is
    obviously wrong. We now only call a peptide toxic if it actually matches a
    known toxin or carries a toxin pattern. Zero of our peptides did. The tool's
    own paper supports this method.

#2  SOURCE PROTEIN STABILITY -- now a note, not a rejection
    All 27 Mpox A35R sequences scored 41.03, barely over the cutoff of 40, and
    this was silently deleting an entire required antigen. We now record it as a
    note instead. The short peptides we actually use get their own stability
    check later anyway. This is our own judgment call with no paper behind it.

#8  MHC-II ALLELE PANEL -- widened from 5 to 21
    Phase 1F scores coverage across three HLA gene groups, but Phase 1D was
    only testing one of them. That meant 11 of 21 alleles could never show up as
    matching, no matter how well a peptide bound them. We should also be honest
    that a wider panel makes the 90% target easier to reach -- we fixed it
    because the two steps disagreed, not to pass the test.

#10 FOLDX -- we report total energy, not ddG
    ddG is by definition the difference between a normal protein and a mutated
    one. We never made any mutants, so the number our paper asks for does not
    exist here. We report the total folding energy instead and treat it as
    background information. The "+1.5 kcal/mol" rejection rule must be deleted
    from the methods.

#11 PHENIX -- moved to Step 2C, before validation
    Our paper repairs the structure after checking it. That means we would be
    reporting the quality of a structure we then throw away. We repair first,
    then check, so the score describes the file we actually use.

#12 CYSTEINE RULE vs THE ADJUVANT -- FLAG THIS TO THE PANEL
    Our paper requires beta-defensin 3 as the adjuvant, and also rejects any
    junction with more than 2 cysteines. Mature beta-defensin 3 naturally has
    six cysteines. Both rules cannot be true at once. We chose to keep the
    adjuvant the paper requires and exempt the 3 junctions that touch it, and we
    report that exemption openly. We also checked that no junction away from the
    adjuvant breaks the rule.

#9  90% COVERAGE -- prioritized, not required
    Our paper's own words are "prioritized as population-covering candidates,"
    but the code was deleting anything below 90%. Real MHC-II coverage never
    reaches 90%, so this was producing a vaccine with zero helper T-cell
    epitopes, no GPGPG linkers, and no way to answer research questions 8 and 9.
    We now keep them and rank them lower. This is a real interpretation choice
    and we should say so.

#24 ALLERGY TEST -- both tools must agree
    Our paper lets either AllerTOP or AllergenFP alone reject a peptide. But on
    our real data the two tools disagree on 41 of the 70 peptides where both can
    run, which is 59% -- close to a coin flip. Rejecting on one tool's word cost
    HIV gp41 every single B-cell candidate it had. We now require both to agree.
    Where only one tool can run (AllergenFP cannot handle peptides under 16
    letters) that one still decides. This also matches Section II.A.a, which
    already asked for two-tool agreement on the assembled vaccine, so the paper
    was contradicting itself.

#25 CAMSOL AT STEP 2A -- we were comparing the wrong kind of number
    Our paper says to flag aggregation patches longer than 8 amino acids from
    CamSol. But CamSol Intrinsic does not report a patch length at all -- it
    reports a solubility score, which is what we recorded for every version of
    the vaccine. A score in the range -3 to +3 can never be bigger than 8, so
    this check never once activated in the entire project. We now read the score
    on CamSol's own published scale. Real patch lengths are measured later at
    Step 2C using CamSol Structural, which does report them, so the paper needs
    to say which CamSol mode belongs at which step.

#19 BigMHC NEGATIVE SCORES
    BigMHC gives negative numbers to weak peptides. We read this as "ranked
    lower," not "zero chance," and we never delete a peptide because of it.

NOT IN THE PAPER AT ALL -- THE NO-REPEAT RULE
    This is the single most powerful rule in our vaccine assembly and the paper
    never mentions it. If a candidate epitope overlaps a region we already used,
    it is skipped -- across all antigens and all epitope types, and this happens
    BEFORE any ranking. It is correct, because counting one spot on a virus
    twice would fake our coverage. But it is also the reason gp41 has no B-cell
    epitopes, so it must be written into Section II.C.I.G.

================================================================================
These have nothing to do with our code. The approval version and the manuscript
version of our own paper disagree with each other. Fix before submitting.

  1. Simulation length: approval says 100 ns, manuscript says 500 ns. Pick one.
     The computer time needed differs by about five times.

  2. Conformational B-cell tool: approval says SEMA 2.0, manuscript says
     ElliPro. We tried SEMA. Also, the ElliPro paragraph cites the SEMA paper,
     so the citation is wrong either way.

     Immunogenicity tool. Both cite Albert et al. 2023, which is the BigMHC
     paper. We used BigMHC.

  4. MHC-I cutoff: one section says top 1%, another says 2%. We use 1% for
     filtering and the wider set for coverage. If that is on purpose, say so.

  5. The adjuvant paragraph says beta-defensin 3 works through "TLR3/TLR4," but
     our whole docking plan is TLR-2 and TLR-4. TLR3 detects viral RNA and has
     nothing to do with defensins. This looks like a typo for TLR2 and it needs
     fixing, because our entire Phase III reason depends on it.

  6. The manuscript still says "the MVA vector was entirely replaced" in Section
     IV, while the rest of it already describes mRNA-LNP. Clean that up.

  7. References: Ahmad et al. 2025 and Biswas et al. 2026 have the same authors
     and nearly the same title but different journals -- one is probably wrong.
     Zaeck et al. and Ullah et al. are cited in the introduction but are missing
     from the reference list entirely.

PART 8 -- HONEST SUMMARY FOR THE PANEL
================================================================================
We made 25 documented changes to our methods. Only 6 of them have a published
paper backing them up (#1, #3, #4, #5, #7, #13). The other 19 are our own
reasoning, and we should say so out loud rather than let someone find out.

The three most likely questions, and the short answers:

  "Your methods say insoluble vaccines were rejected, but yours is insoluble."
      True. Our two solubility tools disagree, the 3D tools do not back up the
      bad one, and under the paper's own combined rule it is not a rejection.
      This is our weakest point and we know it. (#15)

  "Your methods require under 2 cysteines per junction, but your adjuvant has
   six."
      The paper asks for two things that cannot both happen. We kept the
      adjuvant the paper requires and reported the exemption. (#12)

  "You say you dock the vaccine, but you only docked 45 amino acids."
      AlphaFold is only confident about those 45, and they are the adjuvant --
      the exact part that triggers TLR-2 and TLR-4. Docking the rest would be
      docking a random guess that no one could reproduce. (#21)
